# Imperfect Textures Make Automated Shorts Feel Human

The most defensible recipe for a human-looking, Vox-style 9:16 short is stepped, slightly rough and restrained. Graphics sit at about **12 fps inside a 24 fps edit**. Cutout edges are roughened rather than clean. One or two cheap texture cues are added (cycled distressed paper, grain applied last at roughly **15-20% opacity**). Music changes about **every 20 seconds** and stays sparse under a voice of about **150 words per minute**. Shot lengths are skewed rather than uniform (**median about 3.6 s** in a vendor sample of machine-built timelines). The evidence is uneven, and the texture evidence is the weakest. No Vox staff member in any gathered source describes grain, paper or boil. Every texture number comes from third-party After Effects tutorials, chiefly PremiumBeat's breakdown of the "Vox look", plus general handmade-look guides. What Vox staff do document is process. Producers own whole videos and build them in After Effects and Premiere. They license APM library music and splice it by hand. They write plain scripts with pauses after big ideas. Vox's own short-form guidance asks for "transient, organic, native" rather than polished. For Remotion, Remotion's own docs pages, as captured in the notes, describe a native `@remotion/effects` feature (from 4.0.464) with `paper()`, `halftone()`, `whiteNoise()`, `roughenEdges()` and `lightLeak()`. That finding is docs-level only; nothing in the notes shows these effects rendering headless. Several note sections are still pending (SVG filters, canvas/GLSL, CSS blend reliability, cutout and draw-on recipes, SFX-from-scratch tooling, music theory, graphic-format builds), and the "Gaps and unverified" section lists them without filling them.

*Tags used below: **Cited** = stated by a linked source (the source class is named where it is weak: tutorial, vendor, opinion). **Inference** = my reading of the evidence, not stated by any source. Many Vox-staff sources date from 2016-2022, and several people quoted (Johnny Harris, Joss Fong, Estelle Caswell, Phil Edwards) have since left Vox, so treat process claims as historical.*

## Vox graphics come from producer-built After Effects templates, not bespoke 3D

**Cited.** Vox's video team began on a "one person owns a video" model. By 2016 it had grown from one person to 11 full-timers who worked like independent YouTubers and shaped the look of their own videos ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/?p=124626)). Estelle Caswell says After Effects is her main tool, that she taught herself from YouTube tutorials, and that she still animates most of her own work. She counted beats and syllables by hand to sync animation to audio, and she treats animation as a comparatively fast, late step after research and writing ([School of Motion podcast](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/podcast/estelle-caswell-vox-interview)). Joss Fong described the black-hole video as cut and mixed in Premiere Pro, with motion graphics in After Effects and Photoshop/Illustrator only occasionally. Every video gets a script editor to flag confusing parts, and she used Google Earth Studio for telescope-site shots with moderate camera moves "to add energy" ([The Open Notebook, Jan 2020](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).

Job postings reinforce the same stack. The Art & Motion Graphics Director role lists After Effects, Photoshop and Illustrator, and owns Vox's video style guides "as tools, templates and rules" ([Built In listing](https://builtin.com/job/art-motion-graphics-director/6985404)). A Vox Media Senior Motion Designer posting on the brand side lists Cinema 4D only as "a plus" ([ShowbizJobs](https://www.showbizjobs.com/jobs/vox-media-senior-motion-designer-in-new-york/jid-g673j8)). No Vox source names Blender, DaVinci Resolve, QGIS, Datawrapper or Flourish.

Two production anecdotes give scale. For the "Kamala Harris, explained in 7 moments" video, the producer turned it around in about one week against the usual three. The art director asked for a **CRT-style jittery filter** as a stylistic choice, and clips were corner-pinned onto monitor screens in After Effects, a 2.5D effect rather than real 3D ([Storybench, Nov 2024](https://www.storybench.org/?p=22821)). A Vox Atlas map episode of about 10 minutes took five weeks. Its workflow was Earth Studio camera path, JPEG sequence into After Effects, a linked master world map, then hand-drawn lines, arrows and labels ([Storybench, Nov 2022](https://www.storybench.org/vox-atlas-producer-sam-ellis-on-his-map-animations/); [Google Earth blog](https://medium.com/google-earth/how-vox-video-uses-earth-studio-for-dynamic-visual-storytelling-703fc871766e)). Two workflows are explicitly post-Vox practice and should not be read as Vox standards. Johnny Harris uses After Effects with the paid GEOlayers plugin and a custom Mapbox base map ([transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/johnnyharris/how-i-make-my-maps)). Phil Edwards builds reusable on-screen text and animation templates and finishes in about five days after script lock ([The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)).

**Inference.** The planning order across sources appears to be research and asset gathering, then a script written around the available visuals, then a Premiere edit with placeholder graphics, then After Effects motion graphics, then audio. No single source lays out that sequence. Because producers animate their own work under a thin art-direction layer, Vox graphics are likely simple, repeatable constructions (masks, shape layers, parenting, templates). That maps naturally onto Remotion components, where a "template" is a React component and a "mask" is an SVG path or clip. It also means a skill does not need 3D to look Vox-like. The graphic-format sections of the graphics notes (maps, charts, lower thirds, how each is built) were never completed, so no verified per-format build recipe exists here beyond the PremiumBeat lower-third described below.

## Texture: stepped 12 fps motion, rough paper edges and last-step grain do most of the work

### The Vox-specific evidence is thin, so the look is reconstructed from tutorials

**Cited.** No Vox art-director interview in the notes names a texture. The closest thing to a Vox-look specification is a PremiumBeat tutorial (author Lewis McGregor, 2021) that reproduces it in five breakdowns. This is a third-party tutorial, not Vox's own process ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/replicating-vox-motion-graphic/)). It prescribes five things. First, render the graphics comp at **12 fps while the main edit stays 24 fps**, so graphics visibly stutter and "add character to plain elements". Second, build lower thirds from a rough textured PNG whose jagged edges are kept, revealed by a keyframed mask that grows unevenly and skips frames, with text revealed the same way a few frames later. Third, build the "motion background" from **five or six distressed textures cycled so two or three appear each second**, duplicated to about 10 seconds behind static content (with only one texture, flip and rotate it; the author points to a free Shutterstock set of 50 distressed textures). Fourth, apply a lens look with the free Quick Chromatic Aberration plugin on a top adjustment layer, kept subtle and slightly rotated, plus **Gaussian Blur 3.5 restricted to the frame edges** by an inverted circular mask feathered about 50. Fifth, build "tracking transitions" by placing two pre-edited sequences as 3D layers and pulling a 3D camera back over the edit point, with Easy Ease peaking on the cut, while an adjustment-layer blur is keyframed up at the cut and down a few frames later, shorter than the camera move. The article does not cover grain, paper, drop shadows, halftone or palette.

Other Vox-adjacent evidence is sparse but useful. Caswell holds on a single image for several seconds and rarely uses transitions, and her interview mentions no grain or paper ([School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/podcast/estelle-caswell-vox-interview)). An aescripts case study of a Johnny Harris volcano map (Harris is no longer at Vox; the page is undated) lists Deep Glow, a Video Copilot Orb and "film grain as a last texture step" ([aescripts](https://aescripts.com/learn/making-maps-for-johnny-harris---volcanoes)). Separately, a 2026 opinion essay argues that polish is now suspected of being AI-made and that visible handmade cues work as a trust signal, while added-on imperfection can be "strategic mimicry" ([roblog](https://roblog.co.uk/2026/02/faking-realness/)). This is opinion, but it frames the risk: texture that is obviously pasted on can backfire.

**Inference.** Taken together, the "Vox look" is not a texture pack. It is plain, flat, well-lit graphics plus a small number of imperfection cues (stutter, rough edges, subtle lens). The skill should therefore treat texture as a handful of parameterised, cheap cues that vary per video, not as a heavy filter on everything.

### Stepped motion: 4-12 fps seed refresh, with 12 fps as the Vox-look anchor

**Cited.** Every tutorial in the notes converges on stepped time, with different rates:

| Technique | Values | Source |
|---|---|---|

**Inference.** The cited range for the stepped refresh rate is 4-12 fps, with 12 as the Vox-look default and 6-8 for a more visible "boil". In Remotion this is a frame quantisation. Hold the seed constant for N frames and derive it with `random()`. The whole-frame rule matters: at 24 fps, a hold of 2 frames gives 12 fps, 3 gives 8 and 4 gives 6. At 30 fps, 12 fps is 2.5 frames, so holds become uneven, and the whole-frame rates are 10 fps (hold 3) or 6 fps (hold 5). That argues for a **24 fps composition**, matching the PremiumBeat 24 fps edit. The notes did not check whether the target platform accepts 24 fps, so confirm that before committing.

### Remotion's native effects package covers much of this, with caveats

**Cited.** Remotion's docs, as read in the notes on 2026-11-12, describe an effects feature available **from 4.0.464**. It takes an `effects={[...]}` array on `<Solid>`, `<HtmlInCanvas>`, `<Video>`, `<Img>`, `<CanvasImage>`, `<AnimatedImage>`, `<Gif>`, `<RemotionRiveCanvas>` and `@remotion/shapes` components. Effects apply in listed order, and `createEffect()` builds custom ones on 2D canvas, WebGL2 or WebGPU ([Effects](https://remotion.dev/docs/effects)). Effects do not animate on their own; props must be driven from `useCurrentFrame()` and `interpolate()` ([lightLeak](https://remotion.dev/docs/effects/light-leak); [whiteNoise](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/effects/white-noise)). The effects the notes confirm by reading their pages:

`effectsOutputSize` exists from 4.0.474 ([Effects](https://remotion.dev/docs/effects)). A `blur({radius: 40})` example and a `halftone-linear-gradient` page also exist, but only the pages in the table were read in full. A third-party news summary advertises about 50 effects, which the notes did not read ([AlphaSignal summary](https://alphasignal.ai/news/remotion-ships-50-video-effects-react-developers-can-control-with-ai.md)). Remotion 5.0 was "not yet released" on its migration page, so everything here targets 4.0.x ([v5.0 migration](https://remotion.dev/docs/5-0-migration)).

I am stating this as a docs-level finding and no more. The notes never ran these effects in headless Chromium. They did not find a stated `--gl` requirement, an install command, or the full effect list. The `--gl` page says `angle` is recommended for WebGL on desktop (default in 5.0), `swangle` is the slow CPU default on Lambda/Cloud Run, `angle-egl` suits Linux GPU instances (4.0.52+), and `angle` fails on GitHub Actions 4.0 runners because they lack a GPU ([--gl option](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/gl-options)). The Performance page warns that CSS `box-shadow`, `text-shadow`, gradients, `filter: blur()` and `filter: drop-shadow()` can be slow on GPU-less machines and suggests precomputed images instead ([Performance](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/performance)). That bears directly on cutout shadows.

**Inference.** The effects are GPU shaders on a canvas, so a working WebGL2 context is likely required in the renderer. Plan a one-frame `npx remotion still` smoke test per target machine. Pin one exact version across `remotion` and `@remotion/effects` (and every `@remotion/*` package, since Remotion requires matching versions). Prefer `noise()` over `whiteNoise()` for film grain, per the docs' own contrast, but its props were not read. The notes do not say whether `paper()` overlays a texture on the source colours or replaces them, so test it before building a template around it.

### Short code recipes (composed from cited APIs, not run headless)

The procedural-recipes note holds no finished snippets. Its SVG-filter, canvas/GLSL, CSS-blend, stepped-animation, cutout, draw-on and compositing sections are all still pending, and only the Remotion-specifics section is filled. The snippets below are therefore **composed by me from the cited API signatures** and are **UNVERIFIED headless**. Each uses only documented functions and props, except where a comment says otherwise.

Recipe 1 is a deterministic stepped seed (the quantised "boil" clock). `random()` takes a string seed and returns the same value on every render thread ([random()](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/random)); the frame-quantising pattern is inference.

```tsx
import {random, useCurrentFrame} from 'remotion';

// Comp at 24 fps: hold 2 -> 12 drawings/s, 3 -> 8, 4 -> 6.
export const useBoilSeed = (key: string, hold = 2) => {
  const frame = useCurrentFrame();
  return random(`${key}-${Math.floor(frame / hold)}`) * 1000; // documented seed ranges are 0-1000
};
```

Recipe 2 is a rough-edged cutout that re-seeds on each held drawing. It uses the documented defaults as the starting point. The import path follows the docs-URL slug pattern of `paper`, `halftone` and `light-leak` and should be verified.

```tsx
import {Img} from 'remotion';
import {roughenEdges} from '@remotion/effects/roughen-edges'; // path assumed from the docs slug

export const Cutout = ({src}: {src: string}) => {
  const seed = useBoilSeed('cutout', 2);
  return <Img src={src} effects={[roughenEdges({amount: 1, border: 26.5, scale: 0.07, seed})]} />;
};
```

Recipe 3 is a halftone treatment using only documented props. The rotation value is illustrative, not from the notes.

```tsx
import {halftone} from '@remotion/effects/halftone';
// <Img src={src} effects={[halftone({shape: 'circle', dotSize: 20, rotation: 15, sampling: 'bilinear', colorMode: 'source'})]} />
```

Recipe 4 is a highlighter draw-on, using the cited `evolvePath` (progress 0 = invisible, 1 = fully drawn) with a 0.3-0.6 s eased reveal ([evolvePath](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/paths/evolve-path); duration and easing from [autoae](https://autoae.online/blog/how-to-make-highlight-text-animation-video)).

```tsx
import {evolvePath} from '@remotion/paths';
import {Easing, interpolate, useCurrentFrame, useVideoConfig} from 'remotion';

export const Highlight = ({d, start}: {d: string; start: number}) => {
  const frame = useCurrentFrame();
  const {fps} = useVideoConfig();
  const progress = interpolate(frame, [start, start + 0.45 * fps], [0, 1], {
    easing: Easing.inOut(Easing.ease), extrapolateLeft: 'clamp', extrapolateRight: 'clamp',
  });
  const {strokeDasharray, strokeDashoffset} = evolvePath(progress, d);
  return <path d={d} fill="none" stroke="#FFE800" strokeWidth={42} strokeLinecap="round"
               strokeDasharray={strokeDasharray} strokeDashoffset={strokeDashoffset} />;
};
```

Recipe 5 is slow camera drift. `noise3D(seed, x, y, z)` returns a smooth value in [-1, 1] ([noise3D](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/noise/noise-3d)). The 1-5% push and pixel range are an inference from the editing notes. Never use CSS `transition` or `@keyframes` for any of this ([Troubleshooting](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/troubleshooting/css-animations)).

```tsx
import {noise3D} from '@remotion/noise';
// inside a component; shotFrames = this shot's length in frames
const t = frame / fps;
const dx = noise3D('drift-x', 0, 0, t * 0.5) * 6;            // px, inference
const dy = noise3D('drift-y', 0, 0, t * 0.5) * 6;
const scale = 1 + 0.03 * (frame / shotFrames);               // 3% push, inference (range 1-5%)
// style={{transform: `translate(${dx}px, ${dy}px) scale(${scale})`}}
```

### A compositing order assembled from the cited pieces

**Inference** (each step cites its source above; the order is mine). Start with flat art on a low-detail, non-pure-white background to avoid banding. Roughen the edges of cutouts and offset any print-style colour layers by **0.5-2 px**. Hold the stepped seed at **12 fps (hold 2 at 24 fps)**. Lay a cycled distressed-paper texture on **Multiply** (five or six textures, two or three visible per second, flipped or rotated if only one exists). Add a soft precomputed shadow rather than a live CSS blur. Add a subtle chromatic aberration and edge-only blur. Apply **grain last**, at about 15-20% opacity, as the Harris volcano case study does. Add a small dither if banding appears. Use texture on a subset of scenes, not all of them.

## Sound: library cues spliced every ~20 seconds and ducked under a 150 wpm voice

**Cited.** Vox video music is chosen by the producers themselves. Joss Fong searches, tries, swaps and edits stock tracks, and Estelle Caswell handles "music content" for Earworm and knows reverb tricks that make a track sound as though it has ended when it has not ([The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)). Fong says Vox licenses its video music from **APM** (Associated Production Music) and looks for minimal, energetic tones. Phil Edwards, a former Vox producer, has used APM for about eight years and builds playlists from new releases and "similar tracks". He uses APM Stems for closing sections and interstitials, APM risers, and Soundsnap (a paid SFX library) for effects. He also treats vocal tracks as occasional gags, since explainers rarely suit vocals ([APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)). Earworm is different: it uses short fair-use clips of existing songs ([School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)). Vox's audio-engineer listing describes mixing, cleanup and loudness standards on the show side, not specifically YouTube explainers ([listing](https://app.joinrise.co/jobs/vox-media-audio-engineer,-voxcom-remote-if89)).

The documented structure comes from one video. In the black-hole piece Fong switched tracks **roughly every 20 seconds**, cut tracks into sections, aligned beats, layered bass or kick from another track, and extended or pasted endings. She used track starts and ends as emotion and attention cues, ended a track right after a key statement for emphasis, kept the music sparse or low under narration, and avoided busy instruments that compete with the narrator's pitch range. She opened with fast music to hook, and placed an angelic choir-style track at the delayed first reveal as a reflective moment. The music edit took a full day ([The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)). Johnny Harris splits music into "thinking" (beat-driven, neutral, for information) and "feeling" (ambient, cinematic, acoustic, for emotion), alternates them for relief, and warns against tear-jerking music before the story has earned it. This is his own channel practice ([Harris](https://rosetta.to/u/johnnyharris/how-i-use-music-thoughts-on-the-creative-power-of-music)). A music-licensing vendor's style table (low-to-medium reliability) gives curious marimba or pizzicato at 95-110 BPM, light tech pulse at 110-124, subtle cinematic underscore at 70-90, ambient pads at 60-75, and playful plucks at 115-128 ([Foxi Music](https://www.foximusic.com/blog/explainer-video-music-background/)).

**Levels and delivery (Cited, mostly third-party).** The same vendor suggests ducking music **-14 to -18 dB with a 300-450 ms fade** (Premiere Essential Sound presets), or -10 to -16 dB with a 400 ms hold (Resolve Fairlight). It also suggests keeping music about 20-25 dB below voice during speech (the 20 dB figure is attributed to WCAG 2.1 without a link) and a music EQ bell cut at **1.5-3.5 kHz, 2.5-4 dB deep, Q about 1.0-1.4** to leave room for the voice ([Foxi Music](https://www.foximusic.com/blog/explainer-video-music-background/)). YouTube normalisation targets about **-14 LUFS** and only turns loud audio down, never quiet audio up, according to a third-party mastering guide (YouTube's own help pages were not retrieved) ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/mastering-for-streaming-platforms.html)). Another guide gives a -15 to -13 LUFS window with true peak at -2 dBTP ([Sage Audio](https://sageaudio.com/articles/mastering-voice-over-dialogue-for-video)), while EBU R128 sets about -23 LUFS for broadcast with a -1 dBTP maximum and does not recommend loudness range as a measure for programmes under one minute ([EBU R128 v4.0](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128v4_0.pdf)). Sage Audio's voice chain is: gate with a **6-8 dB range**, high-pass at up to about **80 Hz**, gentle bell cuts around 300-800 Hz, a 1-2 dB cut at 5-10 kHz, split-band de-esser under about 4 dB reduction, compression at about 2:1 to 4:1 with under 6 dB gain reduction, and a limiter last with under 6 dB.

**Sound effects (Cited, generic motion-sound guidance; no Vox source beyond Soundsnap and APM risers).** Align the loudest transient, not the file start, to the frame where the action lands, and nudge a hit one or two frames earlier if the action peaks after contact. Slightly early reads better than slightly late ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video)). Lead with the whoosh and land the impact on the end state ([Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/audio-and-final-rhythm-using-sound-to-support-motion); [Pixflow](https://pixflow.net/blog/enhancing-motion-graphics-with-cinematic-transition-sounds/)). Stagger layers by roughly **one to three frames** so the ear fuses them, for example a sharp mid-range attack, then a heavier low layer 1-3 frames later, then reverb ([Sound Ideas](https://sound-ideas.com/blog/Enhancing-Sound-Effects-by-Slightly-Offsetting-Diverse-Sound-Elements)). Keep SFX in one family and use a starter kit of one whoosh, one hit, one tick and an optional riser (3-5 accents total). Hierarchy is voice, then brief SFX accents, then music bed ([Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/audio-and-final-rhythm-using-sound-to-support-motion)). Shift a cue so its peak avoids an important word ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video)). Candidate sources exist for paper and scribble sounds ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-category/materials-textures/paper-sound-effects); [Floraphonic](https://floraphonic.itch.io/pencil-write-and-draw-foley); [Raw Ambience](https://rawambience.itch.io/writing-scribble-sfx)), but their licences were not checked.

**Inference.** The Vox sound is the generic production-music idiom (minimal, energetic, narration-friendly), edited tightly. It is a shared editorial taste, not a single composer's voice, which makes it easy to imitate. Scaled to a 55-70 s short, the 20-second cadence suggests **two to three music sections** (hook, body, close), with a track end or silence on the key claim. The levels conflict: the ducking presets (-14 to -18 dB) and the unlinked "20-25 dB below voice" figure disagree, and my own working range of about 12-20 dB below voice peaks, ducked further under dense passages, is unsourced. Treat all three as starting points and verify with a LUFS meter and a low-volume listen. For SFX, a Vox-style vocabulary maps to graphic events. Use a soft swish for slides and camera moves, a tick or pop for highlights and text, a paper rustle for documents and photos, a pencil scribble for hand-drawn underlines, key taps for typed text, a shutter for photo snaps, a low hit for reveals, and a riser into a reveal. In a 24-30 fps cut, whoosh transients **1-3 frames (about 40-100 ms) early** and the impact on the landing frame or one frame early is a working range to test by ear, not a sourced rule. Synthesising a whoosh as band-passed noise with an envelope plus a short tonal tick and one shared reverb bus is a cheap original recipe, but the notes' open-tooling section on this (Q7) is still pending, so no tool or licence has been verified.

## Pacing: skewed shot lengths, planned pauses and vertical-first framing

**Cited.** No Vox average shot length or cuts-per-minute figure exists in the notes. The only quantitative timeline data is a vendor's sample of 33 AI "Auto Edit" long-form projects (3-20 min, 4,412 scenes). The median scene is **3.62 s** (3.70 s weighted per video), the middle half of per-video medians is 3.30-4.32 s, and the percentiles are 10th 1.54 s, 25th 2.30 s, 75th 5.25 s and 90th 7.46 s. The first 30 s runs faster (median 3.20 s versus 3.70 s after), 18.4% of scenes are under 2 s and 1.3% are over 12 s, and the median video is 9.1 min with 128 scenes (mid-teens per minute). The vendor says the data reflects how timelines were built and does not prove any length improves retention ([OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/how-often-should-visuals-change-youtube-video)). Its opinion-level starting ranges are 2-4 s for a fast hook, 3-6 s for a dense explainer, 6-10+ s for an important photo or piece of evidence, and 5-12+ s for a chart to read. Voice-over runs about **150 words per minute** (2.5 words per second), with 60-second scripts of about **140-150 words**, according to an animation-studio vendor article ([Breadnbeyond](https://breadnbeyond.com/how-many-words-does-a-60-seconds-explainer-video-needs/)).

Vox's script rules are documented at the craft level. Narration should be plainer than print and not too clever, because viewers process picture and voice at once. Pauses go after big ideas, with signposting that points viewers at what to look at, and a script editor reviews every video. Fong opened the black-hole video on the telescopes before the blurry image so the reveal would not underwhelm ([The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)). Vox managing producer Valerie Lipinski said a working headline should steer the story from the start, and the same panel advised beginning from what the audience does not yet understand ([ONA](https://newsroom.journalists.org/2019/09/13/a-good-explainer-video-explained/)). Writers were often brought in only to narrate scripts written by the video team, so the voice is often a colleague, not an announcer ([The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)).

For short-form, Vox's then VP of Video said in Nov 2022 that TikTok and Shorts should feel "transient, organic and native" because polished pieces can read as ads, and that Vox reuses graphics from editorial projects where the subject fits ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/voxs-short-form-video-strategy-has-a-money-problem-but-fulfills-publishers-civic-duty/)). A Vox Media vertical-video job post has one person owning pitch, record, edit, captions and graphics, with several quick-turn videos a week ([Greenhouse](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/voxmedia/jobs/6978982)). Platform safe areas are not officially published for organic Shorts. A vendor estimates, at 1080x1920, top about 120-290 px, bottom about 300-670 px, right about 96-190 px and left about 48 px, leaving a usable **984x1500 px** core. The same vendor suggests (without cited evidence) captions of 60-75 px, one to three words at a time for karaoke style, and a width under about 850 px ([BlitzCut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-size-youtube-shorts-2026)). Tella's safe-zone docs give the same qualitative advice ([Tella](https://www.tella.com/docs/help/editing/social-safe-zones.md)).

Human-made signals are mostly process and personality. Edwards draws on camera in unconventional materials, keeps mishaps and credits sources openly ([The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)). Fong printed data visualisations on paper to walk viewers through a vaccine video ([Storybench](https://www.storybench.org/?p=24033)). Caswell's Earworm used custom illustrations, props and stop animation ([The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)). For legally clean imagery, NASA content is generally not copyrighted but logos, third-party material and identifiable people carry limits ([NASA](https://nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media)). The Library of Congress leaves rights determination to the user and the per-item statement ([LoC](https://loc.gov/legal)). The Internet Archive does not guarantee copyright status ([Internet Archive](https://help.archive.org/help/rights/)). Prelinger films are not blanket public domain ([Prelinger](https://help.archive.org/help/prelinger-archive/)).

**Inference.** A healthy shot-length distribution is right-skewed: many 2-4 s shots, a tail to 8-12 s for evidence and charts, and a few sub-2 s accents. A uniform shot length is a statistical tell of automation. A 55-70 s short at the vendor's mid-teens scenes per minute gives roughly **13-18 scenes**, but that sample is long-form and Shorts are probably faster, which no source measures. At 2.5 words per second a 55-70 s script is 137-175 words before pauses, and each second of deliberate pause removes about 2.5 words. The documented lever of "pauses after big ideas" translates, for an automated voice, to **0.4-0.9 s of extra silence after the claim-bearing sentence**, varied sentence length and no metronomic cadence. Both numbers are mine, not sourced. For charts and text, hold time should be at least words divided by about 3 plus about 1 s. Hard cuts should dominate, with a transition only at section changes, consistent with Caswell's rare use of transitions. Cheap-looking tells and their pro alternatives (same transition on every cut, one music bed throughout, text animating on every line, charts held 2 s, no on-screen source lines) are inference, not sourced findings. The human-feel levers (a 1-5% push on stills with randomised direction, animation durations varied 0.25-0.6 s, 0.5-1.5 s of silence before a reveal, on-screen source lines) are likewise unsourced craft knowledge. An automated QA gate that measures shot-length variation, repeated transition types, music-change count, text words versus hold time, and loudness is the natural way to enforce all of this.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. A Vox-like look is mostly process and restraint. Short tutorial-sourced imperfection cues (12 fps stutter, a rough alpha edge, cycled paper, last-step grain) sit on top of plain, legible graphics, a skewed cut rhythm and music that mostly stays out of the voice's way. Nothing in the evidence says Vox relies on heavy texture. The sources that do prescribe textures also warn against using them everywhere. The practical implication is that the skill should randomise and then measure variation (shot length, music-change timing, seed refresh, texture subset) rather than chase a single signature filter. The strongest "automated" tells in the notes are uniformity: identical shot lengths, one transition, one bed.

The new native effects in Remotion could cut a lot of custom shader work, but only the docs have been read. The first engineering task is to render one frame each of `paper()`, `roughenEdges()` and `halftone()` on the actual target machine under each `--gl` mode before committing a template to them. Until then, the stepped-seed, draw-on and drift approach (recipes 1, 4 and 5) is the lower-risk base, since it depends only on `random()`, `interpolate()`, `@remotion/noise`, `@remotion/paths` and standard SVG, not on GPU shaders.

# Vox explainer craft: script structure, narration, editing/pacing, vertical layout, human-made detail, and clean sourcing

Scope note: all findings are paraphrased. Labels used inside findings: **[STATED]** = a named person or the source itself says it directly; **[MEASURED]** = numbers the source author computed from their own data; **[OPINION]** = a vendor/blogger heuristic with no cited evidence. Anything inferred from watching videos or from general craft knowledge is confined to the "Inferences" subsections and is not sourced. Research date: 2026-11-12. Several primary pages were unreachable by the fetch tool (see Gaps in each section).

## 1. Script structure and what Vox staff say publicly about their writing process

### Takeaway
Public statements from Vox video staff describe a visual-first, research-heavy, editor-reviewed process: pick a working headline early, assume the viewer knows nothing, write plainer than print, leave pauses after big ideas, and have a second person find confusing passages. I found no public Vox style guide or hook taxonomy, so hook types and the question-then-answer arc below are inference, not Vox statements.

### Cited Findings
- **[STATED]** Valerie Lipinski (managing producer, Vox Video) said at ONA19 that a working headline should steer the story from the start, and that creativity should be kept up through the whole process. The panel's general advice was to begin with what the audience does not yet understand. — [ONA Student Newsroom, "A good explainer video, explained" (2019)](https://newsroom.journalists.org/2019/09/13/a-good-explainer-video-explained/)
- **[STATED]** Imaeyen Ibanga (AJ+, not Vox) on the same panel: do not assume background knowledge; test is whether a newcomer can follow while an expert still gets value. — [ONA Student Newsroom (2019)](https://newsroom.journalists.org/2019/09/13/a-good-explainer-video-explained/)
- **[STATED]** Irene Noguchi (Vox's Today, Explained podcast) said varying music, pacing and narrative style carries over to audio explainers; her team set a dry gerrymandering segment to a rock-anthem-style track (a deliberate tonal mismatch/humor choice). — [ONA Student Newsroom (2019)](https://newsroom.journalists.org/2019/09/13/a-good-explainer-video-explained/)
- **[STATED]** Script rules from the Vox black-hole video write-up: video narration needs simpler phrasing than print; the script should not be too clever or too beautiful because viewers process picture and voice at the same time; pauses after big ideas give people time to absorb them; script editors review every video and flag confusing sections because authors lose perspective on their own text. — [The Open Notebook, "Videogram" (Jan 2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Structure decision in the same video: it opens on the telescopes (scale and distance of the instrument) before showing the blurry black-hole image, so the fuzzy picture does not feel underwhelming; the reveal was delayed and paired with a reflective musical moment. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** That video was an update of a ~2015-16 Vox video built on a Brian Resnick article, revamped in advance of an announcement and published about an hour after the press conference. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** The producer admitted the video had assumed the team would image Sagittarius A* when they actually imaged M87, and the labeling only partly corrected this; the article describes no formal fact-check step. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Early Vox format (Joe Posner): an "animated opinion essay" pairing expert interviews with animation; Posner's test was that a story belongs on video only if it has distinctly visual elements. Joss Fong shifted the team to writing her own scripts and bringing writers in only to narrate (first major hit: a 2014 Shark Week debunk). Fong later added editorial structure by reviewing scripts and writing a training tutorial on crafting explainers. — [The Long Story, "Why the best journalists on YouTube are all former Vox employees"](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)
- **[STATED]** Staff were expected to do the whole pipeline (research, script, narration, edit, animation); Fong called it a "jack of all trades" model that was less efficient but gave ownership. Estelle Caswell front-loaded research and primary-source interviews for her Earworm music series. — [The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)
- **[STATED]** Phil Edwards (Vox alum, now on his own channel per the article): visuals drive the writing, because available footage, documents and photos shape the script. He spends roughly the first week of a two-week cycle on research, asset collection and writing; hunts for a "Rosetta stone" source (e.g., a dissertation or a 1914 medical article); treats research as done when facts start repeating across documents; credits sources in the video description. Videos run 9-15 min. — [The Explainers, "Making the trivial...nontrivial"](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)
- **[STATED]** Edwards tests ideas by making a thumbnail and headline and showing them to a private group of YouTube creators; he says a low-performing video (wheelies, under 50K views) had warning signs he ignored. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)
- **[STATED]** Edwards adds narrative interest with fourth-wall breaks, side quests, backstories, interruptions, visual gags and layered audio. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)
- **[STATED]** Joss Fong (Howtown): about half of a six-week production cycle is research (papers, interviews with researchers); videos are 15-20 min; she is a self-described stickler for facts and adds nuance to avoid oversimplified claims. — [Storybench interview](https://www.storybench.org/?p=24033)
- **[STATED]** Vox video team context (2017): about 60% of pieces were the video team's own ideas, ~5 YouTube videos a week, staff grew 12 to 20 in 2016; Posner said some stories need depth and would be damaged by cutting length. Examples: a ~6-minute North Korea piece, a ~10-minute climate piece; "Syria's War: A 5-Minute History" passed 110M views across YouTube and Facebook. — [Digiday (May 2017)](https://digiday.com/media/vox-com-tripled-youtube-viewership-one-year/)
- **[STATED]** Digiday (2017): average watch time was about one minute three years earlier; half of viewers now watched at least four minutes. — [Digiday (May 2017)](https://digiday.com/media/vox-com-tripled-youtube-viewership-one-year/)

### Inferences
- *[Inference, not sourced]* Because Vox writes around available visuals, an automated pipeline should lock the asset list (photo/footage/document/graphic per beat) before the final script, not after.
- *[Inference, not sourced]* "Establish scale/context first, then reveal" is a repeatable structural move: a context shot or two, a delay, then the payoff with a music change.
- *[Inference from general explainer craft]* Typical Vox-style arc: concrete hook (odd fact, visual puzzle or contradiction) → plain statement of the question → 3-6 explanatory steps, each introduced by what the viewer would wonder next → a complication/counterpoint → an ending that returns to the opening image or phrase. This is a common pattern, not documented by Vox in the sources I could fetch; treat as a hypothesis to test.
- *[Inference]* A mandatory "naive viewer" review pass (a second reader or a second LLM pass told to flag every undefined term or logical jump) mirrors Vox's script-editor step.

### Gaps
- No Vox-authored script template, hook taxonomy, typical script word count, or section-length rule was found.
- Vox's fact-checking process is not described in any source I could open (Long Story mentions fact-checkers only for Cleo Abram's later channel).
- Rosetta (rosetta.to) transcript pages of Vox/Kurzgesagt talks appeared in search results but were not fetched in this pass.

## 2. Narration: words per minute, tone, pauses, emphasis, recording

### Takeaway
No Vox-specific words-per-minute figure was found in citable form. What is documented: plain phrasing, deliberate pauses after big ideas, writers/producers voicing their own scripts, and (at Howtown, Fong's current channel) breaking narration with unscripted conversational segments. Numeric WPM targets below must be treated as assumptions to verify.

### Cited Findings
- **[STATED]** Narration should be simpler than print and not showy, with pauses after big ideas. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Under Fong, writers were brought in mainly to narrate scripts written by the video team, i.e., the voice is often a colleague, not a trained announcer. — [The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)
- **[STATED]** Howtown breaks scripted narration with unscripted conversation recorded on Riverside, where one host acts as an audience stand-in asking clarifying questions. — [Storybench interview](https://www.storybench.org/?p=24033)
- **[STATED]** Edwards's on-camera/narration persona: self-deprecating, light "dad humor", openly shares mishaps (e.g., keeping a shot after his camera fell), which the article says makes nerdy interest feel permissible. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)

### Inferences
- *[Inference]* The documented human-sounding levers are: plain syntax, pauses placed after the key claim (not at random), and a personality with opinions and small admissions. For a TTS/automated voice this implies: insert 0.4-0.9 s extra silence after the sentence carrying the main claim, vary sentence length, allow an occasional fragment, and avoid uniform cadence. (Specific second values are my suggestion, not from a source.)
- *[Inference]* Recording conversationally (host + stand-in asking a naive question) is a way to inject natural rhythm; an automated analogue is a short "Q from the viewer" line in the script.

### Gaps
- No Vox figure for WPM, recording setup, mic/room, or take-editing method (breath removal, pickups) was found.
- Search results pointed to generic WPM pages (voxbooster, virtualspeech, lettercounter, Deepgram's educational-YouTuber analysis) but the Deepgram page redirected away and the others were not verified; the Guo/Kim/Rubin (2014) edX study on speaking rate and engagement could not be fetched (not on the tool allowlist).

## 3. Editing metrics: shot length, cuts per minute, hold times, music cadence

### Takeaway
There is no published Vox average-shot-length or cuts-per-minute figure. The only quantitative timeline data I found comes from a vendor's own sample of AI "auto edit" projects (median scene ~3.6 s, right-skewed), which shows what machine-built timelines look like and explicitly does not prove viewer retention. Vox's own documented pacing lever is musical: tracks swapped roughly every 20 seconds.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEASURED, vendor sample, not Vox, not about retention]** 33 long-form projects (3-20 min) built with the vendor's Auto Edit tool, 4,412 timed scenes: median scene 3.62 s pooled (3.70 s with each video weighted equally); middle half of per-video medians 3.30-4.32 s; percentiles 10th 1.54 s, 25th 2.30 s, 75th 5.25 s, 90th 7.46 s; first 30 s median 3.20 s vs 3.70 s after (22 of 33 videos faster at the start). Distribution: 18.4% under 2 s, 37.9% 2-4 s, 24.8% 4-6 s, 10.9% 6-8 s, 6.6% 8-12 s, 1.3% over 12 s. Median video 9.1 min with 128 scenes (~mid-teens scenes/min). — [OverseerOS blog](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/how-often-should-visuals-change-youtube-video)
- **[OPINION, same source]** Starting ranges for main-visual change in narrated long-form: fast hook 2-4 s; standard faceless narration 3-5 s; dense explainer 3-6 s; documentary storytelling 4-7 s; important photo/evidence 6-10+ s; chart or diagram to read 5-12+ s; practical start of 12-20 main scenes/min. The source states its data does not show any scene length improves retention. — [OverseerOS blog](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/how-often-should-visuals-change-youtube-video)
- **[STATED]** In the Vox black-hole video, editors changed music tracks about every 20 seconds, cut tracks into sections and combined them with added beats, avoided busy instrumentation that competes with voice-over, and used the start and end of tracks as emotional/attention cues; sourcing and editing music could take a full day. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Gentle camera movement on aerial footage (Google Earth Studio) was used in the same video to add energy to otherwise static place shots; motion graphics were built in After Effects (with Photoshop/Illustrator) and everything was cut in Premiere Pro. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Edwards builds animated timelines and uses 3D/Unreal Engine sparingly for specific gags and finishes the shoot/edit/animate/publish stage in about five days after scripting. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)

### Inferences
- *[Inference from the vendor distribution]* A healthy shot-length distribution is right-skewed: many 2-4 s shots, a long tail up to 8-12 s for evidence/charts, a few sub-2 s accents. A uniform shot length (every shot 3.0 s) would be a statistical tell of automation. Rule candidates: set a target median ~3.5-4 s for standard narration but sample lengths from a skewed distribution (e.g., lognormal), and bind length to content type (chart/evidence longer; transitions/lists shorter).
- *[Inference]* Hold times should scale with reading time: on-screen text/charts need at least (words / ~3 words per second) plus ~1 s; the vendor's 5-12+ s chart range is consistent with this. This is a reading-speed heuristic of mine, not a Vox statement.
- *[Inference]* Music beds in 15-30 s sections with changes tied to topic shifts, ducked under VO, match the documented ~20 s Vox cadence.

### Gaps
- No Vox ASL, cuts/min, text hold time, or chart hold time figure was found; no academic ASL study specific to explainers or Shorts was located. Search returned mostly vendor blog posts (Opus, Buffer, ClipFlip, joyspace) on short-form length, which I did not open and which are low-evidentiary-quality.
- Cinemetrics-style academic shot-length literature was not searched in this pass.

## 4. Vertical format (9:16): Vox adaptation, safe areas, text size, captions

### Takeaway
Vox treats vertical as a quick-turn, native-feeling format made by the same producers who make YouTube videos, reusing existing graphics and animation where they fit, not as polished re-edits. Platform safe areas are not officially published for organic Shorts, so numbers are vendor estimates; a centered ~984x1500 px core is the common working rule.

### Cited Findings
- **[STATED]** Vox's VP of Video & TV (Emily Anderson, Nov 2022) said TikTok/Shorts content should feel transient, organic and native rather than polished (polished pieces can read as ads); Vox reuses graphics/animation from editorial projects when the subject fits TikTok; no dedicated TikTok team, YouTube producers also make TikToks; return on Nov 1, 2022 with a post every few days. — [Digiday (Nov 2022)](https://digiday.com/media/voxs-short-form-video-strategy-has-a-money-problem-but-fulfills-publishers-civic-duty/)
- **[STATED]** Vox Media job post (Senior Producer, Shortform Vertical Video): several quick-turn vertical videos a week; one person owns pitch, record, edit, captions, graphics, platform optimization and publishing; occasional on-camera appearances with personality; Premiere/After Effects/Illustrator plus CapCut and Descript familiarity. — [Vox Media careers (Greenhouse)](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/voxmedia/jobs/6978982)
- **[Vendor estimate; the source says YouTube publishes no official organic Shorts safe zone]** At 1080x1920: top ~120-290 px, bottom ~300-670 px, right ~96-190 px, left ~48 px; usable ~984x1500 px centered; Google's published vertical *ad* safe zone is cited as 288 px top, 672 px bottom, 192 px right. — [BlitzCut caption size guide](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-size-youtube-shorts-2026)
- **[OPINION, unsourced in that article]** Captions 60-75 px (65 px default), hard max ~100 px, 1-3 words at a time for karaoke style, 3-8 words per card for short-phrase, 8-15 words per card for full sentences; keep caption width under ~850 px and roughly 700-1,000 px above the bottom edge. — [BlitzCut caption size guide](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-size-youtube-shorts-2026)
- **[STATED]** Tella's safe-zone overlay documentation advises keeping titles, faces and key visuals in the central area, away from the bottom action bar and right-side buttons (overlay lists Reels, TikTok, Shorts, LinkedIn). — [Tella docs](https://www.tella.com/docs/help/editing/social-safe-zones.md)

### Inferences
- *[Inference]* Vox's "reuse graphics from long-form" approach implies long-form graphics are designed with enough padding to be re-cropped to 9:16 (single focal element, large type, few simultaneous labels). For original work: design vertical-first scenes with one idea per frame, not shrunken 16:9 layouts.
- *[Inference from watching, unverified]* Vertical explainers from Vox, Johnny Harris, Wendover, Kurzgesagt and Half as Interesting appear to use big burned-in captions, a single visual subject, and tighter cuts than their long-form; I have no sourced measurements.

### Gaps
- No sourced analysis of how Vox, Johnny Harris, Wendover, Kurzgesagt or Half as Interesting reframe graphics for 9:16, nor their shots/min in Shorts. No official YouTube/TikTok/Instagram pixel safe-zone documents were fetched.

## 5. Craft details that read as human

### Takeaway
Documented "human" signals are mostly about process and personality: hand-made props and drawn-on-camera visuals, visible mixed media (printed charts on paper, small apartment shoots, stop-motion), openly credited sources, honest mishaps and corrections, and sound/music chosen by trial and error. No source in this pass specifically describes camera drift, imperfect alignment or varied animation easing; those are inferences.

### Cited Findings
- **[STATED]** Edwards draws on camera with unconventional materials (e.g., barbecue sauce on kraft paper), keeps visible effort and personal mishaps in the finished piece, and credits sources openly; he says he improved lighting, editing, sound design and on-screen text templates over about nine years. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)
- **[STATED]** Fong printed data visualizations on paper to walk viewers through a vaccine video step by step; Howtown productions mix animation, small on-location or apartment shoots and data viz; editing and sound design are done in-house and Fong finds editing the hardest stage. — [Storybench interview](https://www.storybench.org/?p=24033)
- **[STATED]** Caswell's Earworm series used heavy pre-production, primary-source interviews, custom illustrations, props and stop animation. — [The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)
- **[STATED]** Vox editors chose music through keyword search and trial and error, spliced tracks, and kept arrangements sparse under the voice; they delayed an emotional reveal and scored it with a reflective passage. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Vox's team learned by shadowing colleagues and online tutorials, with executives largely leaving them alone to experiment; the team avoided chasing Facebook-native video. — [The Long Story](https://thelongstory.substack.com/p/why-the-best-journalists-on-youtube)
- **[STATED]** Visual material at Vox included newsreel footage, animated graphics and graphic overlays alongside narration. — [Digiday (2017)](https://digiday.com/media/vox-com-tripled-youtube-viewership-one-year/)
- **[STATED]** Edwards uses public-domain archival reports, charts and old footage as core material. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)

### Inferences
- *[Inference, labelled as unsourced craft knowledge]* Human-feel levers an automated pipeline can imitate cheaply: (1) slow push/pan (1-5% scale over the shot) on stills with randomized direction and speed; (2) 1-3 px/0.5-2 degree misregistration on hand-drawn-style annotations; (3) animation durations varied 0.25-0.6 s with eased curves rather than one global timing; (4) room tone or tape/paper foley under archival visuals; (5) occasional deliberate silence (0.5-1.5 s) before a reveal; (6) on-screen source lines ("Source: ... , year") for every archive/chart; (7) one or two humor beats (visual gag or aside) per 3-4 minutes.
- *[Inference]* Corrections culture (a visible correction or an on-screen "earlier version said..." note) is itself a human signal; Vox's own producer discussed a labeling error openly.

### Gaps
- No source describing Vox camera-drift conventions, handwritten annotation fonts, sound-design libraries, or fact-checking tooling.
- No interview with Estelle Caswell, Carlos Maza, Coleman Lowndes or Adam Cole on sound design was located in this pass.

## 6. Common mistakes that make automated or amateur explainers look cheap, and what pros do instead

### Takeaway
I found no authoritative source cataloguing "cheap explainer" mistakes. The closest evidence is the Vox/Open Notebook craft guidance (avoid busy music, don't over-write, pace reveals) and the vendor data showing machine timelines cluster tightly around ~3-4 s. The checklist below is therefore mostly inference.

### Cited Findings
- **[STATED]** Pros avoid busy instrumentation competing with narration, change music about every 20 s, and use track starts/ends as attention cues (versus one looping bed). — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[STATED]** Pros do not over-polish the script; plainness is intentional. — [The Open Notebook (2020)](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[MEASURED, vendor sample]** Auto-edited timelines have 75-80% of scenes between ~1.5 and 7.5 s and median near 3.6 s; the vendor warns this reflects how timelines were built, not viewer response. — [OverseerOS blog](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/how-often-should-visuals-change-youtube-video)
- **[OPINION]** Vertical-video vendors stress keeping text inside platform safe zones and sizing captions large; no evidence cited. — [BlitzCut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-size-youtube-shorts-2026)

### Inferences
- *[Inference]* Checklist of tells and pro alternatives:
  - Uniform shot length → skewed lengths bound to content type.
  - Same transition (crossfade/zoom) on every cut → mostly hard cuts, with a transition only at section changes.
  - Stock-looking, generic B-roll → specific documents, maps, archival footage, screenshots with visible source lines.
  - Text that animates on every line → text static or simple fade; animate only the element that matters.
  - One music bed all video → section-level music changes, ducked under VO, silence at key reveals.
  - Metronomic voice with no pauses → pauses after claims, varied sentence length.
  - Charts held for 2 s → hold for reading time plus a beat.
  - No sources on screen → source lines and description credits.
  - Facts stated with uniform confidence → hedged where uncertain, with one correction/limitation note.
- *[Inference]* Add an automated QA gate that measures: shot-length coefficient of variation, count of repeated transition types, music-change count, text-on-screen word count vs hold time, and sources per factual claim.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or industry study on why audiences judge explainers as automated/cheap was found; "retention and editing studies" requested in the brief were not located (search returned vendor blogs).

## 7. Sourcing and legally clean imagery

### Takeaway
"Free to use" is not "no checking needed." NASA, Library of Congress, Smithsonian Open Access, Wikimedia Commons and Internet Archive each have specific carve-outs (logos, identifiable people, third-party material, per-item rights, uploader-asserted licences). Unsplash and Pexels use their own licences, not CC0, and do not guarantee model/property releases. Build a per-asset ledger: URL, author, licence, date accessed, caveat flags.

### Cited Findings
- **NASA** [STATED]: content (images, audio, video, 3D models) is generally not copyrighted in the US and may be used for educational/informational purposes; NASA logos/insignia (Meatball, Worm, Seal) are protected and need separate approval for merchandise/promotion; no use implying NASA endorsement; some material belongs to third parties and must be cleared with the owner; commercial use of an identifiable person may need their permission (current employees/astronauts cannot be used in commercial products or advertising); NASA should be acknowledged as source. — [NASA Images and Media guidelines](https://nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media)
- **Library of Congress** [STATED]: the Library generally does not own rights to collection items; users must decide for themselves whether an item is under copyright or public domain; users are responsible for privacy/publicity rights; some items contain personally identifiable information; the per-item "Rights and Access" statement is where status is described. — [LoC Legal](https://loc.gov/legal)
- **LoC "Free to Use and Reuse"** [STATED]: staff-curated themed sets (new set roughly monthly) of items the Library believes are public domain, have no known copyright, or were cleared by the owner; stated as belief, not a guarantee, so check each item; dataset covering 2,610 curated items was made May 2023, updated March 2024. — [LoC Labs Free to Use](https://data.labs.loc.gov/free-to-use/)
- **Internet Archive** [STATED]: it does not guarantee copyright status or vouch for rights information on item pages; rights data usually come from the uploader or a chosen CC licence; use is "at your own risk"; many items are limited to non-commercial use with attribution. — [Internet Archive Help: Rights](https://help.archive.org/help/rights/)
- **Prelinger Archives (on Internet Archive)** [STATED]: not blanket public domain; reuse depends on the CC licence shown on each film's page; titles with uncertain or live copyright were withheld (post-1964 works largely absent); the catalogue descriptions/shotlists are not free for commercial use without permission; credit is requested, not required (suggested wording: archival footage supplied by Internet Archive); the page does not address third-party content inside films such as music. — [Internet Archive Help: Prelinger Archives](https://help.archive.org/help/prelinger-archive/)
- **Unsplash / Pexels / Pixabay** [secondary-source summary, verify against licence text]: each uses its own licence, not CC0 (Unsplash dropped CC0 in 2017, Pixabay in 2019; Pexels has some older CC0 items); free commercial use and modification are allowed; compiling content into a competing service is prohibited; none guarantees model or property releases, and the platforms do not verify uploader rights; Pexels states responsibility for determining needed permissions rests solely with the user; the summary page does not address video for Unsplash. — [LicenseOrg, "Free Stock Photos Aren't as Free as You Think"](https://licenseorg.com/blog/free-stock-photos-licensing-traps)
- **Smithsonian Open Access** [title-level only, page not fetched]: the Creative Commons post titled "Smithsonian releases 2.8 million images + data into the public domain using CC0" is dated 2020-02-27 per its URL; search listings elsewhere cite a later, larger count (4.5 million), so the current total is unverified. — [Creative Commons post](https://creativecommons.org/2020/02/27/smithsonian-releases-2-8-million-images-data-into-the-public-domain-using-cc0/); [My Modern Met](https://mymodernmet.com/smithsonian-open-access-public-domain)
- **Vox practice** [STATED]: Edwards credits sources in video descriptions and builds on public-domain archival reports, charts and footage. — [The Explainers](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)

### Inferences
- *[Inference]* Safest asset hierarchy: (1) US-government works (NASA, many LoC items, NOAA, USGS) after checking for third-party flags; (2) items with explicit CC0/public-domain marks (Smithsonian, Commons PD/CC0, Met Open Access); (3) Commons CC BY / CC BY-SA only with full attribution and awareness that share-alike may propagate; (4) Unsplash/Pexels only for non-people, non-trademark scenes; (5) Internet Archive only when the item page shows a CC licence or clear public-domain mark.
- *[Inference]* For every asset, store a ledger row and burn a short credit line on screen or in the description; flag any asset containing recognizable people, logos, buildings, artwork, or music.

# Music and Sound Design of Vox Explainer Videos (and how to reproduce the feel with original/royalty-free/synthesized audio)

_Status: DRAFT v1 (partial, written early; extended below as research continues). Researched 2026-11-12. Everything is paraphrased. "Cited Findings" = stated by a source. "Inferences" = my synthesis or inference from listening/knowledge, not stated by a source._

## Q1. Who composes or supervises music and sound at Vox video?

### Takeaway
Public evidence shows Vox video music is chosen and edited by the video producers themselves (e.g., Joss Fong, Phil Edwards), with Estelle Caswell named as the Vox staffer responsible for "music content" (the Earworm series). No named staff composer for the YouTube explainers turned up; Vox audio engineers exist mostly on the podcast side (job listings).

### Cited Findings
- Joss Fong (senior video producer, founding member of Vox's video team) described her own music workflow in a 2020 Open Notebook interview: she searches, tries, swaps and edits stock tracks herself, rather than commissioning a composer. — [The Open Notebook, Videogram: Vox black hole video](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- In the same interview Fong describes Estelle Caswell as the person who handles Vox's music content and says Caswell knows reverb-tweaking tricks that make a track sound as if it has ended when it has not. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- Phil Edwards (Vox video producer, started as a scriptwriter, "wears all the hats", edits his own pieces) picks and cuts his own music; he uses Adobe tools and Descript for audio cleanup. — [APM "Meet Your Storyteller": Phil Edwards](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Edwards states that Vox editors are encouraged to borrow techniques from commercials and trailers; he says many colleagues also edit. — [APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Estelle Caswell (Earworm) does most research, writing and animation herself, in After Effects (largely self-taught from YouTube tutorials); she notes After Effects is not designed for editing to audio, so syncing animation to rhythm was manual (counting beats/syllables). Earworm leans on short fair-use music clips and experts. — [School of Motion: Estelle Caswell interview](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- Vox Media audio-engineer job listing (voxcom, remote) describes mixing, sound design and cleanup across shows with producers, choosing music that supports the story, and tools like Pro Tools, iZotope RX, remote-recording platforms, EQ/compression/loudness standards; reports to a Director of Audio Engineering. This is an audio (podcast/show) role, not specifically YouTube explainers. — [Vox Media audio engineer listing (via Rise)](https://app.joinrise.co/jobs/vox-media-audio-engineer,-voxcom-remote-if89)

### Inferences
- The Vox YouTube pipeline appears to be producer-driven: the person who cuts the video also scores it from a library, with specialist music help concentrated in Caswell's Earworm series. A "Vox sound" is therefore a shared editorial taste (minimal, energetic, narration-friendly library cues, cut tightly) rather than one composer's voice.
- The reverb "fake ending" trick is a cheap way to build custom endings out of library cues; it is directly reproducible (see Q7).

### Gaps
- No public interview with a named Vox sound designer/mixer for the YouTube explainers; no credits listing a staff composer. Vox YouTube pages were not fetchable (proxy), so description/credit lines could not be checked directly.
- Netflix "Explained" (Vox Media) composer not found in searches.

## Q2. Original vs library music

### Takeaway
Stated: Vox video licenses music from the APM (Associated Production Music) library, which all Vox Media outlets use; producers edit tracks heavily. No source found for original scoring in the YouTube explainers, and no evidence of Epidemic Sound/Artlist/Musicbed use by Vox itself.

### Cited Findings
- Fong: Vox licenses its video music from APM, a stock-music library shared across Vox Media; producers search by keyword and sift many tracks looking for minimal, energetic tones. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- Edwards: has used APM for about eight years, likes one library across platforms; builds playlists from APM new releases, composer searches and the "similar tracks" feature for videos that typically run 8 to 15 minutes; prefers fresh tracks and avoids reusing; named go-to APM artists include Laurent Dury, Cristof De Jean, Time and Motion and The Acoustic Machines. — [APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Edwards uses APM Stems (separate instrument groups) for more control, including closing sections or building interstitial moments, plus APM risers; sound effects came from Soundsnap (a paid SFX library) in the piece discussed. — [APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Because explainers rarely suit vocals, Edwards looks for chances to use vocal tracks as a music cue/gag (e.g., doo-wop vocals in transitions of one piece; a pop vocal cue synced in a triple-axel video). — [APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Earworm differs: it uses short clips of existing songs under fair use for analysis. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- Context from a former Vox presenter: Johnny Harris describes using Musicbed in a sponsored video, noting artists are paid per use; the page does not tie this to Vox. — [Johnny Harris, How I Use Music (mirror)](https://rosetta.to/u/johnnyharris/how-i-use-music-thoughts-on-the-creative-power-of-music)

### Inferences
- Vox music = mostly licensed production-music (APM), mixed with APM stems/risers and edited, plus SFX library material. The 2020 and later-year interviews may not reflect current practice (APM statement is from 2020; the Edwards piece is undated in my fetch).
- Because APM tracks are broadcast-library cues, the "Vox sound" is the generic production-music idiom (see Q3), which is easy to imitate with original material.

### Gaps
- No confirmation of what Vox uses in 2025-2026 (any shift to Epidemic/Artlist/AI music unknown). No source for Vox commissioning original scores for the YouTube explainers.

## Q5. Sound effects

### Takeaway
No Vox source describes its sound-effect mix in detail (only that SFX came from Soundsnap and that APM risers are used). General motion-sound guidance converges on: one short, same-family SFX per graphic event; align the loudest transient (not the file start) to the frame where the motion lands, lead with the whoosh and land the hit on the end state; sit a hit or tick one to two frames early rather than late; layer 2-3 sounds from different frequency ranges with few-frame offsets; keep SFX below voice and above or equal to the music bed in importance.

### Cited Findings
- Vox-specific: the only SFX source named is Soundsnap (paid library) in the piece Edwards discussed, plus APM risers; see Q2. No Vox source lists paper, pen, typewriter, or shutter SFX. — [APM interview](https://music.copyright.com/blog/meet-your-storyteller/meet-your-storyteller-phil-edwards-of-vox-media)
- Timing: start a whoosh slightly before the transition and land the impact exactly on the new state or contact frame; for a reveal, a small tick at the start and a soft hit when it finishes; only the phrase "a few frames" is given, no precise number. — [Cursa, Audio and Final Rhythm](https://cursa.app/en/page/audio-and-final-rhythm-using-sound-to-support-motion)
- Timing: align the sound's transient (loudest attack), not the start of the file, to the action frame; if the action peaks a frame or two after contact, nudge the effect one or two frames earlier; slightly early tends to read better than slightly late, and one frame early can feel tighter for sharp impacts; no millisecond values given. — [Morphic, how to sync sound effects](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video)
- Timing: put the strongest part of a transition sound on the exact keyframe where the motion completes or changes direction; secondary layers may sit a few frames off to add depth while the main accent stays clearly defined. The article gives no frame count. — [Pixflow, cinematic transition sounds](https://pixflow.net/blog/enhancing-motion-graphics-with-cinematic-transition-sounds/)
- Layering: stagger layers by roughly one to three frames (or less) so the ear fuses them into one event; example of a sharp mid-range attack, then a heavier low-frequency layer 1-3 frames later, then reverb; fade earlier layers so they do not mask the next attack. — [Sound Ideas blog](https://sound-ideas.com/blog/Enhancing-Sound-Effects-by-Slightly-Offsetting-Diverse-Sound-Elements)
- Layering: pair a subtle movement sound (whoosh) with a stronger impact at the emphasis point, and choose layers in different frequency ranges so they do not fight. — [Pixflow](https://pixflow.net/blog/enhancing-motion-graphics-with-cinematic-transition-sounds/)
- Cohesion: choose SFX from the same family so they share brightness and character; do not mix a long-echo hit with a very dry click unless the contrast is deliberate; when several elements move together, one shared accent is often enough; a starter kit is one whoosh, one hit, one tick, and an optional subtle riser (3-5 accents total). — [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/audio-and-final-rhythm-using-sound-to-support-motion)
- Levels (qualitative only): hierarchy of voice first, key SFX accents second but brief, music bed last; duck music under narration and raise it slightly in pauses; if impacts distort, reduce those clips first; check the mix at low volume. — [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/audio-and-final-rhythm-using-sound-to-support-motion)
- Levels: keep SFX on their own track, lower effects slightly under speech, or shift a cue a few frames so its peak does not land on an important word; no dB values given. — [Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video)
- Source material for the categories asked about exists as commercial and indie packs (examples, license not checked for each): paper sound effects category on A Sound Effect; writing and scribble packs on Artlist; "Pencil Write and Draw Foley" by Floraphonic; "Writing Scribble SFX" by Raw Ambience. — [A Sound Effect: paper](https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-category/materials-textures/paper-sound-effects), [Floraphonic on itch.io](https://floraphonic.itch.io/pencil-write-and-draw-foley), [Raw Ambience on itch.io](https://rawambience.itch.io/writing-scribble-sfx)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE (from the tool-neutral sync advice above plus how After Effects/Premiere work): in a 24/30 fps cut, put whoosh transients 1-3 frames (about 40-100 ms) before the visual lands, and the impact/click transient on the landing frame or 1 frame early. Treat that as a starting range to test by ear, not a sourced Vox rule.
- INFERENCE: a Vox-style SFX vocabulary for the formats in the graphics notes would be: soft whoosh or swish for slides and camera moves; tick/click or pop for highlights and text appearing; paper rustle or slide for documents and photos entering; pen/pencil scribble for hand-drawn underlines and circles; typewriter or key taps for kinetic text/typed headlines; camera shutter for photo "snap" cuts; low hit or sub-boom for reveals and scene starts; riser into a reveal. These map directly to the categories in the user brief; the mapping is mine, not a Vox statement.
- INFERENCE: since the ear fuses layers within a few frames, a good cheap recipe is one noise-based whoosh (band-passed noise with an envelope), plus one short tonal tick or low thump, plus one tail of room reverb, all from the same room-tone/reverb bus (see Q7).
- INFERENCE (working numbers to test, not sourced): SFX accents peak a few dB below the voice peaks, and well above the music bed during the cue; keep sustained SFX (scribbles, typewriter) lower than one-shot hits, and keep them out of the 1.5-3.5 kHz speech band by EQ where possible (Q6 gives the sourced music carve).

## Q6. Loudness, delivery, voice-over chain and pacing

### Takeaway
Two reference points: EBU R128 for broadcast (about -23 LUFS integrated, true peak at or below -1 dBTP) and the de-facto online target of about -14 LUFS with 1-2 dB of true-peak headroom, where YouTube turns loud audio down but does not turn quiet audio up. Voice-over pace for explainers is around 150 words per minute per vendor guides; the voice chain is gate, high-pass, gentle EQ, split-band de-ess, light compression, limiter. I found no Vox-published loudness spec.

### Cited Findings
- EBU R128 (current edition fetched: v4.0): programme loudness normalised to about -23.0 LUFS; where hitting the target exactly is impractical (live), up to +/-1.0 LU deviation is allowed; maximum true peak in production is -1 dBTP (+/-0.3 dB tolerance), with stricter limits possible for lower-bitrate distribution; loudness range is not recommended as a measure for programmes under one minute. — [EBU R128 v4.0 (PDF)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128v4_0.pdf)
- YouTube: normalization targets about -14 LUFS, is always on, applies per video rather than per album, only lowers louder audio, and does not raise quiet audio. This is from a third-party mastering guide, not YouTube's own documentation (not retrieved). The same guide's general advice for lossy streams is at least 1 dB true-peak headroom. — [iZotope, mastering for streaming](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/mastering-for-streaming-platforms.html)
- A second guide for video voice-over gives a YouTube window of about -15 to -13 LUFS with true peak at -2 dBTP (noting encoding can shift level by up to 2 dB), and says audio around -20 LUFS stays quiet because YouTube will not boost it. The same page lists Netflix at -27 LKFS +/-2 LU (dialogue-gated) with true peak at -2 dBTP. It is slightly inconsistent internally (window vs. a -14 conclusion). — [Sage Audio, mastering voice-over dialogue for video](https://sageaudio.com/articles/mastering-voice-over-dialogue-for-video)
- Peak-ceiling sources therefore differ: -1 dBTP (EBU; vendor guide below) vs -2 dBTP (Sage Audio) for online video; both exist in the literature, so pick -1 to -2 dBTP. — [EBU](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128v4_0.pdf); [Sage Audio](https://sageaudio.com/articles/mastering-voice-over-dialogue-for-video)
- Voice-over processing chain, in order (Sage Audio): gate with a 6-8 dB range (a large range clips breaths and word ends); high-pass at up to about 80 Hz; gentle bell cuts around 300-800 Hz for clarity and a 1-2 dB cut at 5-10 kHz for sibilance; split-band de-esser under about 4 dB reduction; compression under about 6 dB gain reduction at roughly 2:1 to 4:1; limiter last, under about 6 dB. Meter with an integrated LUFS meter and a true-peak meter. — [Sage Audio](https://sageaudio.com/articles/mastering-voice-over-dialogue-for-video)
- Music under voice (vendor blog, medium-low reliability): keep music about 20-25 dB below the voice during speech (the article attributes the 20 dB figure to WCAG 2.1 without a link); voice-over about -16 to -18 LUFS; final true peak at or below -1.0 dBTP. Ducking presets quoted: Premiere Essential Sound duck amount -14 to -18 dB with 300-450 ms fade; Resolve Fairlight Ducker -10 to -16 dB with 400 ms hold. A music EQ bell cut at about 1.5-3.5 kHz, 2.5-4 dB deep, Q about 1.0-1.4 makes room for the voice; avoid pad build-up at 250-500 Hz. — [Foxi Music, explainer music guide](https://www.foximusic.com/blog/explainer-video-music-background/)
- Pace: average voice-over is about 150 words per minute (2.5 words per second); well-performing 60-second scripts are about 140-150 words; around 240 wpm is physically possible but sounds forced; pace differences come from emphasis and pauses. Vendor article (animation studio), low-medium reliability. — [Breadnbeyond](https://breadnbeyond.com/how-many-words-does-a-60-seconds-explainer-video-needs/)
- Vox-side on pacing: Joss Fong says voice-over needs pauses for big ideas and clear signposting of what to look at (see graphics_pipeline.md section 1 for the cited source), and Vox music is used sparse/low under narration (Q4). — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- Vox audio staff are expected to know EQ, compression and loudness standards (job listing; podcast/show side, see Q1). — [Vox Media audio engineer listing](https://app.joinrise.co/jobs/vox-media-audio-engineer,-voxcom-remote-if89)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE: YouTube-first delivery means mix the final to about -14 LUFS integrated (acceptable -16 to -13), true peak -1 to -2 dBTP, and do not chase louder: louder files are simply turned down. Dialogue-forward explainers can go quieter than music tracks (voice-over around -16 to -18 LUFS short-term with music and SFX beneath), but verify with a meter on the final bounce.
- INFERENCE: R128 -23 LUFS is a broadcast/Netflix-style target; use it only if delivering to TV or a platform that asks for it, not for YouTube.
- INFERENCE: a conservative original-video default is VO at about 140-155 wpm with deliberate 0.3-0.8 s pauses before big ideas; Vox's actual wpm is not documented. It can be measured from caption files with timestamps for videos you study (count words divided by minutes).
- INFERENCE: the 20-25 dB "music under voice" figure is aggressive for an energetic bed; for explainer-style beds a smaller gap (roughly 12-20 dB below voice peaks, ducked further under dense passages) is common practice in my experience, not a sourced number, so verify by ear and by the low-volume check from Q5.
- INFERENCE (recording chain, not sourced): large-diaphragm or dynamic mic 10-15 cm from the mouth with a pop filter, a treated or soft-furnished room, peaks around -12 to -6 dBFS at 24-bit/48 kHz, then the processing chain above. I did not retrieve a source for this recording-side advice.

### Gaps
- No Vox-published loudness target or mix specification (YouTube or Netflix Explained). No Vox source for VO words per minute.
- YouTube's own help pages were not retrieved (fetch limits); the -14 LUFS figure rests on third-party guides. Peak ceiling guidance conflicts (-1 vs -2 dBTP).
- No source retrieved for microphone and room recording practice.
